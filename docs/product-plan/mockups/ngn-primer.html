1. Why NGN exists

The NCLEX exam used to be mostly multiple-choice: read a scenario, pick the right answer. But real nursing isn't a multiple-choice task — it's a sequence of decisions made under pressure, with incomplete information. The old exam tested knowledge. The new exam tests clinical judgment.

NCSBN built a framework called the Clinical Judgment Measurement Model (CJMM) — six cognitive steps a nurse goes through when caring for a patient. Every NGN case study walks through these six steps in order.

Step 1
Recognise cues
What matters in this picture?
Step 2
Analyse cues
What could it mean?
Step 3
Prioritise hypotheses
Where do I start?
Step 4
Generate solutions
What can I do?
Step 5
Take action
What will I do?
Step 6
Evaluate outcomes
Did it help?

Steps 1–3 are cognitive (teal). Steps 4–6 are action-oriented (amber). Every NGN case study hits all six steps in order.

2. Two structural formats

NGN questions come in two shapes on the exam:

Unfolding case study

One patient scenario, six questions. Each question tests one of the six CJMM steps, in order. As the student progresses, new information may appear in the patient's chart.

  • Q1 — Recognise cues
  • Q2 — Analyse cues
  • Q3 — Prioritise hypotheses
  • Q4 — Generate solutions
  • Q5 — Take action
  • Q6 — Evaluate outcomes

Stand-alone items

A single question with its own mini-scenario. Two types exist:

Bow-tie
Drag 5 tokens into a bow-tie diagram: 2 actions, 1 condition, 2 parameters to monitor.
Trend
Data changing over time (vitals, labs). Recognise the pattern, answer using any other NGN item type.

This is a Highlighting item. The passage has pre-defined selectable chunks (not free-form text selection). The author marks which chunks are correct and which are wrong. Partial credit with penalty for wrong picks.

4. The stand-alone bow-tie

Bow-tie is the signature NGN stand-alone item — a single question (no 6-question unfolding), but structurally the most elaborate single item. The student drags 5 tokens into three slots: two actions to take, one condition, and two parameters to monitor.

Bow-tie asks the student to demonstrate clinical judgment in a single elaborate interaction. The student has to synthesise cognitive steps 3, 4, and 6 in one item.

Case studies are the wrapper. Inside them (and in stand-alone items), questions use these interaction patterns:

SATA (Select All That Apply)

6. What this means for building MyNclex

Taking this apart:

  • Every NGN item type has the same metadata shell — stem, rationale, classifications, difficulty, tags. What differs is the content structure (options, rows, tokens, text-spans, etc.) and the correct-answer shape.
  • Case studies are a separate wrapper — they group 6 regular questions around a shared scenario and chart data. Each question inside is just a regular item with a case_study_id and an order.
  • Scoring changes — NGN uses partial credit on most item types. The old all-or-nothing model doesn't apply to Matrix, Highlight, Bow-tie, etc.
  • Charts are a new first-class thing — case studies need a "patient chart" concept with multiple tabs (Nurses' Notes, Vital Signs, Labs, Orders) and the ability to unfold (new data appearing between questions).

The schema question this drives

Every item type needs a different data shape. Three ways to store this:

  1. Wide table — add columns for every possible field across every type. Most are NULL. Ugly.
  2. Child tables per type — base + type-specific child. Clean but many tables.
  3. JSONB content column — one base table with a JSON payload per type. Flexible, Postgres-native, no migrations for new types.

Option 3 (JSONB) is probably the answer for MyNclex — this is exactly the kind of polymorphic content Postgres/Supabase handles well.

This document is a reference primer explaining how NGN works. It is not a design document. See mynclex/docs/product-plan/bank.md for the MyNclex schema plan.
